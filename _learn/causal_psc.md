<section id="one">
<div class="inner">
	<header class="major">
		<h2>From observed outcomes to causal statements</h2>
	</header>
	<p>
		Randomised controlled trials remain the gold standard for causal inference, but they are costly and often impractical when only experimental-arm data exist — for example in single-arm trials, registries, or early-phase studies.
	</p>
	<p>
		<strong>Personalised Synthetic Controls (PSCs)</strong> — described as <em>Model Estimated Controls (MEC)</em> in our published methodology paper — address a distinct setting: a cohort of patients who all received an experimental treatment is compared against <strong>counterfactual evidence</strong> generated by a validated parametric model of standard care.
	</p>
	<p>
		For each treated patient, the model estimates what their outcome would have been under control. The gap between the <em>observed</em> experimental outcome and the <em>model-predicted</em> control outcome is used to estimate treatment efficacy.
	</p>
	<ul class="actions">
		<li><a href="https://bmcmedresmethodol.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12874-025-02540-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="button special">Read the published paper</a></li>
		<li><a href="../framework/mathematical_fwk.html" class="button">Mathematical framework</a></li>
	</ul>
</div>
</section>

<section id="potential-outcomes">
<div class="inner box-round">
	<header class="major">
		<h2>The potential-outcomes framework</h2>
	</header>
	<p>
		PSCs are grounded in the potential-outcomes notation of Imbens and Rubin. Consider patients indexed \(i = 1, \ldots, N\) exposed to treatment \(Z \in (0, 1)\). Each patient has two potential outcomes, \(Y_i(0)\) and \(Y_i(1)\), but only one is ever observed.
	</p>
	<p>
		At a population level, causal inference typically targets the <strong>Average Treatment Effect (ATE)</strong>:
	</p>
	<p>
		\[
		\delta = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tau_i
		\]
	</p>
	<p>
		where \(\tau_i\) is the individual treatment effect. In practice, attention often focuses on the <strong>Conditional Average Treatment Effect (CATE)</strong> within prognostic covariates \(X\):
	</p>
	<p>
		\[
		\tau_i(x) = E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0) \mid X = x_i] = m_{1i}(x) - m_{0i}(x)
		\]
	</p>
	<p>
		with \(m_{1i}(x) = E[Y_i(1) \mid X = x_i]\) and \(m_{0i}(x) = E[Y_i(0) \mid X = x_i]\). Other estimands — such as hazard ratios or odds ratios — can be obtained by altering this contrast.
	</p>
</div>
</section>

<section id="psc-estimand">
<div class="inner box-round">
	<header class="major">
		<h2>The PSC estimand</h2>
	</header>
	<p>
		Most causal tools compare <em>expected</em> outcomes under treatment and control using data from both arms. PSCs differ because the observed cohort contains <strong>only experimental-arm patients</strong>.
	</p>
	<p>
		Under <strong>strong ignorability</strong> — potential outcomes independent of treatment given covariates \(X\):
	</p>
	<p>
		\[
		Y(1), Y(0) \perp Z \mid X
		\]
	</p>
	<p>
		and assuming <strong>consistency</strong> (the observed outcome equals the potential outcome under the received treatment), the experimental-arm expectation is observed directly:
	</p>
	<p>
		\[
		m_{1i}(x) = E[Y_i(1) \mid X = x_i, Z = 1]
		\]
	</p>
	<p>
		The counterfactual control expectation is generated from a <strong>Counterfactual Model (CFM)</strong>:
	</p>
	<p>
		\[
		m_{0i}(x) = E[Y_i(0) \mid X = x_i, Z = 1] = f(x_i, Z = 1)
		\]
	</p>
	<p>
		where \(f(\cdot)\) is the CFM applied to each patient's covariates. The PSC treatment-effect estimand is then:
	</p>
	<p>
		\[
		\tau_i(x) = m_{1i}(x) - f(x_i, Z = 1)
		\]
	</p>
	<p>
		Because both the observed and predicted outcomes are available for each individual, PSCs can in principle estimate <strong>patient-level treatment effects</strong>. Whether those individual estimates are reliable depends on the credibility of the assumptions below.
	</p>
	<p>
		Both components are conditional on \(Z = 1\), so the estimand is closest to the <strong>Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT)</strong>. Under exchangeability assumptions, it can also support inference about the ATE. Population-level or subgroup effects are obtained by averaging or restricting \(\tau_i(x)\) over the cohort of interest.
	</p>
</div>
</section>

<section id="how-different">
<div class="inner">
	<header class="major">
		<h2>How PSCs differ from other causal tools</h2>
	</header>

	<div class="box-round">
		<h3>Compared with G-computation and TMLE</h3>
		<p>
			G-computation and Targeted Maximum Likelihood Estimation typically compare <em>expected</em> responses under each treatment using data from both arms. PSCs instead compare an <strong>observed</strong> experimental response against an <strong>expected</strong> control response from the CFM.
		</p>
		<p>
			Conceptually, the CFM can be viewed as an imputation tool for the missing potential outcome \(Y_i(0)\), analogous to the missing-data perspective in Imbens and Rubin — but PSCs add a likelihood for the efficacy parameter and a Bayesian procedure to propagate uncertainty from the model.
		</p>
	</div>

	<div class="box-round">
		<h3>Compared with classical synthetic controls</h3>
		<p>
			Like synthetic controls, PSCs generate counterfactual evidence synthetically. In practice, however, classical synthetic controls form a weighted combination of donor units, whereas PSCs predict each patient's control outcome from a parametric model — enabling <strong>personalised</strong> comparisons.
		</p>
	</div>

	<div class="box-round">
		<h3>Compared with propensity-score methods</h3>
		<p>
			Propensity-score approaches require patient-level data on <em>both</em> treatment groups to balance confounders. PSCs are designed for settings where only the experimental arm is observed, provided a transportable CFM of standard care is available.
		</p>
	</div>
</div>
</section>

<section id="assumptions">
<div class="inner box-round">
	<header class="major">
		<h2>Assumptions required for causal interpretation</h2>
	</header>
	<p>For \(\tau_i(x)\) to support causal conclusions, the following must be plausible:</p>
	<ul>
		<li><strong>No unmeasured confounders</strong> — all variables that affect both treatment selection and outcome are captured in the CFM covariates.</li>
		<li><strong>Stable unit treatment value (SUTVA)</strong> — a patient's potential outcome is not altered by the setting in which treatment was observed.</li>
		<li><strong>Correct model specification</strong> — the CFM adequately describes outcomes under control for the comparison cohort.</li>
		<li><strong>Transportability</strong> — it is reasonable to apply the CFM, developed in one setting, to the cohort being compared.</li>
		<li><strong>Overlap</strong> — each experimental patient had non-zero probability of receiving control.</li>
		<li><strong>No extrapolation</strong> — inference is not performed beyond the covariate range supported by the CFM.</li>
	</ul>
	<p>
		Uncertainty from the CFM itself must also be accounted for in estimation; treating model parameters as fixed overstates precision. The <code>psc</code> R package uses Bayesian resampling from the CFM posterior to address this.
	</p>

<section id="evidence">
<div class="inner box-round">
	<header class="major">
		<h2>What the evidence shows</h2>
	</header>
	<p>
		A simulation study comparing PSCs with G-computation and propensity-score overlapping weights found <strong>comparable bias and coverage</strong> across continuous, binary, and survival outcomes when confounding was known. Under unmeasured confounding, no method fully removed bias — as expected — though PSCs showed competitive performance for binary and survival endpoints.
	</p>
	<p>
		In pancreatic cancer, a CFM for gemcitabine (built from ESPAC-3) was used to compare gemcitabine plus capecitabine patients from ESPAC-4. The PSC hazard ratio (0.78; 95% CrI 0.59–1.03) agreed closely with the direct randomised comparison within ESPAC-4 (0.79; 95% CI 0.65–0.96), with appropriately wider uncertainty reflecting model-based controls.
	</p>
